History of Capoeira

Born out of resistance to transatlantic slavery in colonial Brazil, Capoeira weaves martial defense, acrobatic agility, improvisation, percussion, and song into a living dialogue inside a circle called the roda.

1. Afro-Brazilian Origins & Resistance (16th–19th Centuries)

Capoeira emerged among enslaved Africans brought to Brazil — predominantly from Central and West Africa, especially peoples speaking Kikongo, Kimbundu, and Umbundu across regions of modern-day Angola and the Kongo basin. Ethnomusicological and oral traditions trace deep affinities with Central African ritual movement arts such as N'golo (the "zebra dance" documented in southern Angola), inverted postures, sweeps, and percussive circle games.

  1. 16th – 18th Century

    Disguising Combat Inside Rhythm & Dance

    Because weapons and open martial training were violently suppressed on sugar plantations and in port cities like Salvador da Bahia, Recife, and Rio de Janeiro, enslaved practitioners concealed strikes behind music, clapping, and continuous swaying footwork (ginga). To outside onlookers it appeared to be recreation; inside the circle, players sharpened timing, balance, sweeps, and lethal headbutts (cabeçadas).

  2. 1605 – 1694

    Quilombos & Palmares

    In runaway free settlements known as quilombos — most famously the federated republic of Quilombo dos Palmares, led by figures such as Ganga Zumba and Zumbi dos Palmares — Afro-Brazilian combat arts matured alongside guerrilla defense against colonial expeditions. Even today, Capoeira songs celebrate Palmares and Zumbi as enduring symbols of liberty.

2. Emancipation, Street Maltas & Criminalization (1888–1930s)

When Brazil formally abolished slavery in May 1888 through the Lei Áurea without economic reparations or employment guarantees, thousands of newly freed citizens gathered in cities like Rio de Janeiro and Salvador. Capoeiristas organized neighborhood mutual-aid brotherhoods known as maltas (such as the famous Nagoas and Guaiamuns in Rio), serving as street protectors, dockworkers, and politically feared enforcers.

Decree 847 · October 11, 1890

The Penal Code Ban on Capoeiragem

Fearing the organized autonomy of Afro-Brazilian neighborhoods, Brazil's newly formed First Republic inserted Articles 402–404 into the 1890 Penal Code, outlawing *"agility and bodily dexterity exercises known under the name of capoeiragem"* in streets and public squares under penalty of prison or exile to islands like Fernando de Noronha.

During decades of policing, practitioners relied on secrecy, cryptic street nicknames (apelidos — a tradition continued across all ten characters in Roda Orbital), concealed straight razors, and warning drum/berimbau cadences like Cavalaria to disperse at the sound of mounted police. Folk heroes such as Bahia's Besouro Mangangá (Manoel Henrique Pereira) became legends for evading ambushes through supernatural mandiga and fearless technique.

3. Mestre Bimba & Capoeira Regional (1920s–1937)

By the early twentieth century, two towering Bahian masters reshaped Capoeira from persecuted street art into respected cultural institutions. The first, Manuel dos Reis Machado (Mestre Bimba, 1899–1974), recognized that without structured pedagogy and public legitimacy, the tradition faced extinction or dilution.

1932 · Engenho Velho de Brotas, Salvador

First Licensed Academy & Sequences

Mestre Bimba fused classical Bahian movements with Batuque (an Afro-Brazilian leg-tripping contest mastered by his father) to create Luta Regional Baiana — today called Capoeira Regional. He introduced eight progressive drill sequences (Sequências de Bimba), upright sparring drills, graduation scarves (lenços), and specialized aerial toss-and-escape training (cintura desprezada).

1937 · Decriminalization

National Cultural Recognition

Moving Capoeira out of back alleys and indoors into an officially registered academy convinced skeptics and earned state patronage. Following a 1937 showcase before President Getúlio Vargas — who later hailed Capoeira as *"the only truly national sport"* — legal prohibition was lifted, opening the way for schools across Brazil.

4. Mestre Pastinha & Capoeira Angola (1941–1981)

While Regional emphasized athletic speed and upright efficiency, Vicente Ferreira Pastinha (Mestre Pastinha, 1889–1981) dedicated his life to safeguarding the ancient African-rooted theater, musical ritual, and low-slung strategy of Capoeira Angola. In 1941, he founded the historic Centro Esportivo de Capoeira Angola (CECA) in Pelourinho, Salvador, dressing students in yellow and black in tribute to Ypiranga Futebol Clube.

In Angola, games open slowly at the feet of the berimbaus (ao pé do berimbau) after a solo narrative song called the ladainha. Players hug the ground with negativas, chamadas (ritual invitations to test trust), ironic humor, and devastating close-range rasteiras and cabeçadas.

5. Capoeira Contemporânea, Maculelê & Global Diffusion

From the 1970s onward, younger generations — led by groups in Rio de Janeiro (such as Grupo Senzala), São Paulo, and Salvador — cultivated Capoeira Contemporânea, blending the speed and high acrobatics of Regional with the cunning floor awareness and musical depth of Angola. Brazilian masters soon carried the art across the Americas, Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Folklore of Santo Amaro da Purificação

Maculelê — The Stick & Machete Dance

Often staged alongside Capoeira exhibitions, Maculelê hails from Bahia's sugarcane region of Santo Amaro. Driven by fast atabaque drumming, dancers strike wooden sticks (grimas) or curved machetes (facões) together on every fourth beat in choreographed duels commemorating courage and resistance — directly inspiring the unlockable machete variant and character Grima in Roda Orbital.

Playful Dialogue · Jogar

Why Capoeira Is "Played," Not Fought

In Portuguese you do not "fight" a roda — you joga ("play") Capoeira. A beautiful game relies on Pergunta e Resposta (question and answer): when one player launches a high Armada, the partner answers cleanly with a ducking Cocorinha or cartwheel Aú without breaking rhythm.

6. Music as the Master: The Bateria & Traditional Toques

Music is not background accompaniment in Capoeira — the lead berimbau dictates tempo, personal style, aggression level, and when a game begins or must halt.

7. Global Heritage & Key Vocabulary

Ginga — The Triangular Root Motion

Meaning "to sway," ginga keeps your weight shifting continuously between front and back legs while guarding your face. Rather than offering a fixed target, a capoeirista launches every kick, sweep, or cartwheel straight out of motion.

Malícia & Mandinga — Cunning and Charisma

Malícia is watchful streetwise intuition — reading a partner's feints, baiting a predictable kick, and choosing when to strike versus when to smile and step aside. Mandinga captures the elusive, theatrical magic that makes skilled players look untouchable.

Vadiação & Floreio — Joy and Acrobatic Flourish

Elder Bahians called Sunday street rodas vadiação ("idle wandering" or playful banter). Floreios — such as Aú, Bananeira, Macaco, and Parafuso — weave beauty and unpredictability into that playful conversation.

Axé — Positive Vital Energy of the Circle

Borrowed from Yoruba-Bahian Candomblé tradition, Axé names the collective spiritual vitality generated when drummers, chorus, clappers, and both players inside the roda move in harmony.
